<p>The War of Horn and Fang was a fifteen year conflict fought between the <a href="Vihr" class="internal-link">Rascy zi Vihr</a> and the Kingdom of <a href="Meadrath" class="internal-link">Meadrath</a> from 851PA until 866PA, constituting the largest and most destructive war between two Ilrakhani powers until the current day.</p> 
<p>Fought primarily over control of the <a href="Uhl" class="internal-link">Free City of Uhl</a> and the strategic Bay of Uhl, then a Vihri protectorate for over a century, the war drew in navies of the Blijgrat League, mercenary companies of northern Urland, powerful rogue mages of both nations, and the chivalric orders of the Gethric faith. Its human cost was staggering, its conclusion inconclusive, and its consequences reshaped the political, religious and academic landscape of the continent for generations. The war is seen by many historians as the defining clash of between the two dominant cultural and religious orders of Ilrakhan: the conservative High Gethric world of Vihr and the increasingly Trinicist and academically progressive world of Meadrath.</p>

<h2>Uhl and the Question of the Bay</h2>

<p>The <a href="Uhl" class="internal-link">Free City of Uhl</a> had been a Vihri protectorate since 735PA, when Count Foster of Uhl accepted the protection of Ras Vormil I against the opportunistic Meadi expansion that had seen Duke Abelard von Kran of Lesmere seize Hasterhall in 719PA. For over a century this arrangement had served Uhls merchant class well enough as the vihri military protection came without the administrative annexation that western expansion had implied. Nevertheless it had never sat easily with Meadrath, whose access to the Torn Sea and control of the southern trade routes through the Sallesand Strait were fundamentally constrained so long as the Bay of Uhl remained under eastern influence. The city itself, one of the three great ports of the Torn Sea alongside Sallesand and Trazlov, commanded maritime access to the entire northern coast and the Bloody Strait beyond. He who held Uhl held the key to continental trade.</p>
<p>The immediate causes of the war are somewhat disputed in the historical record. What is clear is that by the 840s PA the strategic pressure had become intolerable from the meadi perspective, and that Arngil II von Kors court had by the end of that decade concluded that a negotiated revision of Uhls status was unavailable and a military solution was the only remaining option. Vihr  under Jerziv I had no intention of surrendering a protectorate it had held for over a century and whose harbour gave the Rascy its only significant maritime outlet toward the west. When Meadi forces mobilized in 851PA, the war that both courts had been preparing for since at least the mid 840s, for better or worse, had finally arrived.</p>

<h2>851PA - 854PA</h2>

<p>The fundamental strategic asymmetry of the war was apparent from its opening weeks. Meadraths forces marched on Uhl through established road networks across the Gisvale lowlands and the Trinbend in a matter of weeks. Vihrs Royal Army faced months of grueling transit through the Pilgrims Pass  before it could reinforce the Vihri garrison at Uhl under Gri Heyran. Arngil II von Kor, who held supreme command of the Meadi war effort and directed it personally from Fort Kranig in Lesmere, moved immediately to exploit this window. A man whose contempt for Vihr was as much personal as strategic, Arngil had spent the better part of two decades building toward this war and had no intention of conducting it cautiously. At the Battle of the Bow in 851PA the Grand Meadi Army defeated Gri Heyrans western forces and opened the land approaches to Uhl. Simultaneously, the Meadi Navy under Duke Thorsten von Firsberg of Mear, reinforced by the combined city fleets of the Blijgrat League, engaged the Uhlian and Vihri fleet in the Bay of Uhl at the Battle of the Bay. A decisive naval engagement in which the allied Meadi fleet shattered the Vihri blockade of Fort Kranig itself and effectively ended Vihrs naval control of the Bay for the remainder of the war.</p>

<p>The momentum faltered almost immediately thereafter, not through Vihri resistance but through internal failure within the meadi forces. The Army of Gisvale, advancing separately to reinforce the Grand Meadi Armys siege of Uhl, was denied the support of the von Kran nobles of Lesmere, whose ancient rivalry with the Archduke of Gisvale proved stronger than the demands of a common war. Marching without that reinforcement, the Gisvale army was defeated at the Battle of Dilfields by the Urien garrison of Fort Dilwatch under Lord Arthur Rensly. Rensly was more than a committed Uhlian nationalist who had never forgiven the Meadi seizure of Hasterhall in 719PA with a fury built by tales of his ancestors. The defeat at Dilfields cost Meadrath the weeks it needed. By the time the Grand Meadi Army had settled into its siege of Uhl, Kisras Miskomir of Vihr had led the Vihri fleet across the Bay from Hagshorn, landed at Jallbreg, and was marching south. The 1st Battle of Tears broke the Meadi siege and forced the Grand Meadi Army into a retreat that, while orderly, left the initiative with Vihr. The main vihri host of the Royal Army, having completed its months long march through the Pass, finally arrived to reinforce the position shortly after.</p>

<h2>854PA - 861PA</h2>

<p>The four years that followed the 1st Battle of Tears were a grinding attritional phase in which Vihr held Uhl and the Bay approaches while Meadrath controlled the western land routes. Neither side possessed the strength to force a decisive resolution. The human cost of this period, measured not in dramatic battles but in sustained campaigning, disease, requisition, and the slow destruction of the Turvi communities caught between the two armies, was arguably greater than the wars more celebrated engagements. The city of Uhl itself, nominally the wars prize, became something closer to its hostage as it was too valuable to abandon but too costly to hold comfortably. Uhls civilian population would end up enduring years of disrupted trade and military occupation with a growing resentment directed equally at both sides.</p>

<p>In 855PA, Meadi forces under Count Warder von Inberg recaptured Gardhil, a fortified town the Vihri had taken in minor skirmishes the previous year, and used it to anchor their eastern defensive line. Von Inbergs conquest was a product of deception as the main Meadi force feigned a northward march toward Dilwatch, drawing half the Vihri garrison out of position to respond, while a small group of agents infiltrated the town, killed local commander Gri Zemon of Ahmsk and collapsed the remaining defences from within. Von Inberg took the town in the resulting chaos. The Capture of Gardhil was a significant morale victory for the meadi forces at a moment when the stalemate was beginning to wear on both armies, and it established the Count as a smart commander who would only grow in influence the wars remaining years to come.</p>

<p>The most consequential development of the stalemate period was not military but institutional. <a href="Muriel von Gloss" class="internal-link">Muriel von Gloss</a>, Countess of Gloss and Rector of Korsfall College, had by approximately 856PA joined the Meadi war effort openly, deploying her magical expertise and academic resources of the Meadi scholarly establishment in direct support of the army in open violation of the <a href="Orenburg Pact" class="internal-link">Orenburg Pact</a> that had governed the neutrality of magical institutions since 817PA. The institutional response was swift and formally severe: in 859PA Muriel was tried by the arcane judiciary, removed from her Rectorship, and censured by the leading magical institutions of both nations. She continued fighting regardless. The personal prestige she had accumulated over decades as Korsfall's most celebrated scholar, and the genuine military value of her contributions to the Meadi war effort, made hera welcome asset to the meadi forces, regardless of what the judiciary had determined. Her presence in the war and the institutions inability to actually remove her from it exposed the Orenburg Pacts fundamental weakness as an agreement whose authority rested on reputational consequence rather than enforcement mechanism, and which could be broken by anyone sufficiently powerful and politically useful.</p>

<p>The autumn of 858PA brought the wars most dramatic single moment to that point. Kisras Miskomir, leading a major combined force of Vihri contingents in an attempt to break the stalemate by striking directly at the Meadi High Command at Orsfurt, was killed in the brutal fighting around the bridge town. For a moment that might have broken the Vihri force entirely, his younger brother Orian took up Miskomirs banner and helmet and led the surviving forces in an ordered retreat before the Meadi could exploit the command collapse. The deception bought Vihr its army. It did not buy Vihr the battle. Arngil II von Kor died that same year at Fort Kranig, having never once set foot on a battlefield despite largely causing the war itself. He was succeeded by his son Gilmar I. Gilmar was but a young man of thirtytwo who inherited the supreme command of a war effort that had just achieved its best year yet and a coalition of powerful commanders: Louis du Jera, Warder von Inberg, the Archduke of Gisvale and other colourful personalities, each with their own interests and ambitions. It was Count Warder von Inberg, the most experienced and politically astute of the Meadi commanders, who recognized the danger of a splintered command and moved quickly to establish Gilmar as the unambiguous single figurehead of the war effort, advising him closely and ensuring the other great lords accepted him not merely as their king but as their commander in chief.</p>
<p>When Ras Jerziv I of Vihr died in the summer of 860PA from a heart attack, his constitution long weakened by gout and his health said never to have recovered from the news from Orsfurt, his son Orian II inherited a war in a questionable position. Orian had lost his father at home and his brother in battle, personally led many engagements clad in armour and had come to know the horrors of war. His counterpart across the field was a decade younger and had never commanded a battle. Neither man had started this war.</p>

<h2>861PA - 866PA</h2>

<p>By 860PA both thrones had changed hands without either side changing course. Orian II of Vihr, in his mid-forties, battle-proven at Orsfurt and many other engagements, commanding the genuine personal loyalty of the men he had led through the worst of the war. Gilmar I of Meadrath, younger, untested in battle, his authority constructed as much by Von Inbergs political skill as by his own. They both found themselves the custodians of a conflict that had already consumed far more than its prize was worth. Both courts understood that the question was no longer who would win Uhl but who would survive the war in better condition to negotiate its end. The military operations of the final phase were conducted in this light, less campaigns of conquest than demonstrations of continued capacity, each side seeking to establish a position of relative strength before the inevitable peace talks.</p>

<p>Meadraths dominant action in this period came at <strong>Felhome</strong> in 861PA, where <a href="Muriel von Gloss" class="internal-link">Muriel von Gloss</a> confronted <a href="Viagoris" class="internal-link">Viagoris</a> , a sorcerer and the most formidable magical practitioner in Vihri service ,in a sustained magical duel of fire and hail that witnesses described as among the most terrifying single encounters of the entire conflict. Muriel prevailed. Viagoris did not emerge from the Field of Tears. His student Perna Ratz, later known as Perna the Mad, gave multiple conflicting accounts of what transpired before her own deterioration rendered her testimony unreliable. Simultaneously, Duke Louis du Jeras forces destroyed the principal urien mercenary companies of the Gryphons Bastards and the Jolly Knights that were accompanying Viagoris in the engagement around Felhome  They were among many such soldiers of fortune drawn into the conflict on both sides throughout the war, whose participation in the fighting around Uhl had swelled the armies of both nations well beyond their standing forces. The eastern forces had lost its most important magical asset and a significant portion of its hired strength in a single afternoon.</p>

<p>Vihrs answer came at Gadly Bridge in 865PA. <a href="Andre" class="internal-link">Andre of Trydlew</a>, the Gri of Trydlew who had emerged as Vihrs most capable field commander in the wars final phase, brought the forces of Duke Louis du Jera to battle at the Gadly crossing and defeated them comprehensively. Inflicting on Meadrath the heaviest single engagement loss of the war and killing Muriel von Gloss in the fighting. She was impaled in the press of battle, aged sixty one, having spent the better part of a decade as the most controversial and most consequential individual on either side of the conflict. Her death silenced the Orenburg debate not by resolving it but by removing its most prominent subject. The institutions that had censured her found themselves unable to agree on what her death meant for the Pact she had violated, a question they debated for years afterward without resolution.</p>

<p>The wars final engagement, the 2nd Battle of Tears in 865–866PA, was one of the largest single battles in Ilrakhani history to that point. The Royal Vihri Army and the Grand Meadi Army committed their full remaining strength to the Field of Tears,  the same ground where Miskomir had relieved the siege of Uhl twelve years earlier, in a collision that lasted multiple days and left both forces too damaged to continue meaningful offensive operations. Meadrath held the field when the Vihri forces retreated toward Uhl, but the strategic implications of the tactical victory were immediately cancelled by the action of the Selbia Brigade under Var Feliks zi Sribo, whose forces had swung wide of the main engagement and fallen on the Meadi supply convoys in their absence, destroying the knight escort, burning the wagons, and leaving the Grand Meadi Army without the logistical basis to press its advantage. Both sides had their victory and neither could use it. Peace talks began before the winter.</p>

<h2>Aftermath</h2>

<p>The <a href="Treaty of Uhl" class="internal-link">Treaty of Uhl</a>, signed in 866PA by Orian II of Vihr and Gilmar I of Meadrath in person at Uhl, the first time the two Monarchs had occupied the same room, alongside the Lord-Mayor of Uhl and the head of the Vrieman Bank, declared Uhl a free city whose independence both powers were bound to uphold and defend. The trade routes through the Sallesand Strait and the Trinmo bend were guaranteed to both signatories. The Vihri protectorate of 116 years was dissolved, and neither power gained what it had fought for: Vihr lost its western maritime anchor, Meadrath failed to absorb the city it had spent fifteen years and incalculable blood attempting to take. Uhl itself, freed from the protectorate that had shaped its identity for over a century, proceeded to grow at a massive rate and the city that neither superpower could own became the wealthiest port on the Torn Sea within generations.</p>

<p><a href="Andre" class="internal-link">Andre of Trydlew</a>, the wars defining Vihri commander, founded the <a href="Knights of the Unicorn" class="internal-link">Knights of the Unicorn</a> three years after the Treaty in 869PA, an institution whose founding reflected the wars legacy of Vihri aristocratic military pride. He died in 874PA, found dead in his quarters under circumstances that were never officially determined, poisoning was suspected but never proven. He had spent the eight years since the Treaty as a celebrated national hero in Vihr, the founder of the Knights of the Unicorn and the man who had killed one of the most hated and powerful sorceresses of the 9th century. The Orenburg Pact, whose authority Muriels actions had exposed as reliant on the willingness of powerful individuals to respect it, survived the war strengthened in its formal provisions and weakened in its practical credibility. The undercurrent of the war, the sense that Trinicist Meadrath and High Gethric Vihr had fought not merely over a port city but over competing visions of what Ilrakhans future should look like, was never formally acknowledged by either court and has never fully subsided.</p>
<p>King Gilmar I von Kor would rule Meadrath until 883PA, Ras Orian II oversaw his Realm until 881PA, neither ruler ever met the other again yet both spoke always with an unusual sense of respect about the other.</p>
