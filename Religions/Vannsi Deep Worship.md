<p>The faith of the <a href="Vannsi" class="internal-link">Vannsi</a> is among the oldest living traditions in the northern world, stretching back beyond recorded history to the ancient deep from which their people first emerged. Practised across all Vannsi cultures of the northern Wyrmrest and Frozen Seas, it centres on a single divine figure known to the Vannsi themselves as Tunthelle, the Deep One, a goddess of the sea in its entirety, worshipped as the source of all Vannsi life and the destination of all Vannsi death. The Vannsi consider themselves her children, born from the primordial waters she inhabits, and regard their existence on land as a long journey that ends only when the sea reclaims them.</p>

<p>Tunthelle is conceived as possessing two aspects, drawn from the eternal rhythm of ebb and tide. In her tidal aspect she is generous, powerful and nurturing; in her ebbing aspect she is vast, arbitrary and beyond mortal reckoning. Neither aspect is regarded as dark or malevolent. The Vannsi, much as the <a href="Aeldcult" class="internal-link">Aeldcult</a> regards <a href="Ingele" class="internal-link">Ingele</a>, understand death not as a tragedy but as a slow and trusting promise — the sea will come for everyone in the end, and this is right. Sea burial is the universal funerary rite, returning the dead to the deep from which they came. Human communities living among the Vannsi are welcomed to participate in these rites, and many Hevri and Jueli families of the <a href="Zren" class="internal-link">Zren</a> have adopted the practice across generations.</p>

<p>Among the human peoples of Trykkerog, Tunthelle is most commonly equated with <a href="Nyllena" class="internal-link">Nyllenna the Saltmother</a>, a figure of the <a href="Bykmali Paganism" class="internal-link">Bykmali Pagan</a> tradition and the broader <a href="Aeldcult" class="internal-link">Aeldcult</a>. The parallels are close enough that scholars and Bykmali alike have long regarded the two as the same spirit under different names, and the Vannsi themselves have generally accepted this interpretation without resistance. To most outside observers, the Vannsi simply worship a vague and ancient "Deep One" of the northern waters, with little understanding of the theology beneath.</p>
