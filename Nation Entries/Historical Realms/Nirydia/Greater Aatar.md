<p>The Greater Aatar was the second unified Shirin empire, established in 281BA following the succession wars that destroyed the <a href="Greater Chihim" class="internal-link">Greater Chihim</a> and ruling the Mondi Delta and river plain for over three and a half centuries. Founded by the Aatar house as an entirely new ruling class displacing the Chihim institutional tradition, the Greater Aatar represents the longest continuous period of unified Shirin governance in recorded history. Its eventual collapse in 83PA, driven primarily by the rising influence of <a href="Lakalism" class="internal-link">Lakalist</a> religious factions whose competing claims on political legitimacy overwhelmed the Aatar's capacity for institutional management, produced the Era of Four Kingdoms from which the <a href="Twintail Imperium" class="internal-link">Twintail Imperium</a> would ultimately emerge.</p>

<h2>Foundation and Early Period (281BA — 200BA)</h2>

<p>The Aatar house that consolidated control over the former Chihim territories by 281BA was a genuinely new political force rather than a reformed continuation of its predecessor. The Aatar rulers deliberately distanced themselves from the Chihim legacy, relocating the imperial capital from the war ravaged Chirat to the newly constructed city of Aataris on the northern river plain, developing a distinct set of administrative titles and court protocols, and replacing the former Chihim governor class with Aatar loyalists wherever possible. This displacement was not merely symbolic. The Aatar understood that the Chihim had been destroyed by the entrenchment of hereditary regional power in the hands of a governor class that ultimately prioritized its own dynastic survival over the empire's coherence, and the early Aatar rulers invested considerable effort in preventing the same structural failure from reproducing itself in their own administration.</p>

<p>The Goara faith remained the official religious framework of the early Greater Aatar, but its relationship to the new ruling house was more transactional than the quasi identification that had characterized Chihim legitimacy. The Aatar presented themselves not as the embodiment of Goaric natural harmony but as its disciplined custodians, a more modest claim that nonetheless served the same legitimizing function without requiring the ruling house to maintain the elaborate theological apparatus the Chihim had built around their person. The Goaric priesthood, having survived the succession wars with its major temple institutions largely intact, accommodated the new rulers with the pragmatic flexibility of an institution that had learned not to bind its survival too closely to any particular dynasty.</p>

<p>The early Aatar period saw significant reconstruction of the river plain infrastructure damaged during the succession wars, the reestablishment of systematic trade networks along the Mondi corridor, and a gradual recovery of the population levels the Chihim had achieved at their height. Two new cities of significance emerged during this period, Prasarp on the southern delta margins and Tonot on the upper river plain, both founded as Aatar administrative centres rather than as organic growths from earlier settlement patterns, and both destined to outlast the empire that created them.</p>

<h2>The Middle Aatar Period (200BA — 120BA)</h2>

<p>The middle period of the Greater Aatar's history is characterized by a stability that, in retrospect, masked the gradual accumulation of structural tensions that would eventually prove fatal. The Aatar administrative system, more sophisticated than the Chihim model it had replaced, maintained genuine central authority over the major provincial centers for the better part of two centuries, a performance that exceeded any of its predecessors. Trade, scholarship, and the material culture of the delta cities all flourished under conditions of sustained peace, and the Aatar court at Aataris became the undisputed center of Shirin intellectual and artistic life.</p>

<p>The Goara faith reached what many scholars consider its philosophical maturity during this period. The major Goaric texts concerning the nature of cyclical time, the relationship between individual conduct and natural balance, and the proper ordering of human communities were composed or systematized in their canonical forms during the second and third centuries BA, largely by scholars attached to the great temple complexes at Aataris and the older centers of Charning-Bardrab and Tiaker. This flowering of Goaric thought would prove to be both the faith's greatest achievement and an inadvertent contribution to its eventual displacement, the precision and accessibility of the newly codified tradition made it available to the critical engagement that would ultimately challenge its foundations.</p>

<h2>The Rise of Lakalism and the Late Aatar Crisis (120BA — 83PA)</h2>

<p>The emergence of what would eventually become <a href="Lakalism" class="internal-link">Lakalism</a> as a distinct religious tendency within the Shirin world dates to approximately 120BA, when the first recognizable articulations of the symmetry principle as a cosmic rather than merely aesthetic concept begin to appear in the scholarly literature of the delta cities. In its earliest form, what would become Lakalism was less a religion than a philosophical position within Goarism itself, an argument that the cyclical natural harmony the Goara faith described was most purely expressed in the mathematical structures of bilateral symmetry, and that Goarism as institutionally practiced had lost its connection to this fundamental principle through centuries of accommodation to political power.</p>

<p>The early Lakalic tendency was genuinely marginal for the first several generations of its existence. Its adherents were concentrated in the scholarly and scribal classes of the delta cities, its arguments were conducted in the rarefied language of theological disputation, and its practical implications for political organization were not yet developed. The Aatar court regarded it with the mild attention it gave to any intellectual novelty that had not yet demonstrated political relevance.</p>

<p>The transformation of Lakalism from a scholarly tendency into a mass movement with genuine political consequences began approximately 60BA, driven by a combination of factors that the Aatar administration proved increasingly unable to manage simultaneously. A series of poor agricultural years produced widespread material hardship across the delta and river plain that the Aatar's granary redistribution system, stretched by decades of population growth, could not fully absorb. The Goaric priesthood's response to the crisis, intensifying ritual practice and attributing the hardship to collective spiritual failure, satisfied neither the scholarly class that had been engaging with Lakalic arguments nor the urban poor whose suffering required practical rather than theological address. Lakalism, with its claim that the present disorder was the consequence of fundamental misalignment between the Aatar's institutionalized Goarism and the true principles of divine symmetry, offered both an explanation and an implicit political program.</p>

<p>The final century of Aatar rule was defined by the increasingly open conflict between Goaric and Lakalic factions within the Aatar administrative and military class. The Lakalic movement had by this period developed the theological sophistication to articulate a complete alternative vision of political legitimacy, one in which authority derived from the ruler's correspondence with divine symmetrical order rather than from hereditary succession or Goaric priestly blessing. This argument was simultaneously an attack on the Aatar ruling house, whose claim to the throne rested on neither of the legitimizing principles Lakalism recognized, and an invitation to any provincial ruler or military commander who could plausibly present himself as the embodiment of the new theological framework.</p>

<p>The Aatar court's attempts to manage this challenge oscillated between suppression and accommodation in ways that satisfied neither the Goaric establishment it was attempting to protect nor the Lakalic factions it was attempting to neutralize. Edicts against Lakalic preaching were inconsistently enforced by provincial administrators who had their own reasons for maintaining relationships with local Lakalic communities. Attempts to incorporate Lakalic language into the official Goaric theological framework were rejected by both the Goaric priesthood, which recognized them as a dilution of its authority, and by the Lakalic movement, which correctly identified them as a co-optation attempt rather than genuine conversion.</p>

<p>By the final decades of Aatar rule the empire had effectively ceased to function as a coherent political entity. The major provincial centers, including Bardrab, Tiakait, Prasarp, and Tonot, were governed by rulers whose primary loyalty ran to their own dynastic and factional interests rather than to an Aatar court that could no longer enforce its will beyond the immediate environs of Aataris. The formal dissolution of the Greater Aatar in 83PA, when the last Aatar ruler's death produced no credible successor and no claimant capable of assembling sufficient support to maintain the fiction of unified governance, ended an institutional tradition that had already ceased to function in practical terms years before its official conclusion.</p>

<h2>Legacy</h2>

<p>The Greater Aatar's most significant legacy for subsequent Shirin history was not institutional but religious. The three and a half centuries of Aatar rule provided the conditions within which Lakalism developed from a marginal philosophical tendency into a mature theological and political system capable of providing the ideological foundation for a new imperial order. The Aatar's failure to resolve the tension between Goarism and Lakalism during the critical period of the latter's growth meant that by the time the Twintail Imperium was founded, Lakalism was already the dominant religious framework across most of the Shirin world, providing Natan I with a suitable legitimizing ideology that required no development, only the political will to claim it definitively. The cities of Prasarp and Tonot, both Aatar foundations, survived the empire's collapse and became two of the four kingdoms from which the Twintail would eventually emerge. Aataris itself declined sharply following the empire's dissolution and never recovered significant political importance, its fate a fitting emblem of the thoroughness with which the Twintail's founders sought to distance their new order from the failures of their immediate predecessor.</p>
