<p>Nabil is a small oasis town in the southern Asef Desert, lying to the south of <a href="Ikara" class="internal-link">Ikara</a> within the bounds of the <a href="Elbae" class="internal-link">Elbae Sultanate</a>. Built around a natural source of water in an otherwise forsaken stretch of desert, the town owes its relative prosperity to the nearby Nabil copper mines, known as the Dustlicker Shafts, which it supplies and serves. Around a thousand people live in the town and its immediate vicinity, making it one of the few permanent settlements of any size between Ikara and the deep south of the Asef.</p>
<p>Though nominally a possession of the Sultanate, Nabil is isolated by the desert that surrounds it, and the presence of imperial authority is felt there far less than in the walled cities of the north. The town has the character of a frontier settlement, a single main street of sun bleached frontages crossed by one other road, where miners, caravaneers, herders and those who prefer to live beyond the easy reach of the Sultan's officials share the same dust and the same water.</p>

<h2>Name</h2>

<p>The name Nabil derives from the old Beheran word for "starlit". The open desert around the oasis offers an unusually clear view of the night sky, and the name is commonly understood as a reference to the travellers who once found the water by night.</p>

<h2>The Oasis</h2>

<p>Nabil exists because of a group of springs that rise just south of the town, where water held deep beneath the Asef is forced to the surface through faults in the bedrock. The springs do not form an open lake. Their water is gathered into stone basins at the source and from there led away through a network of narrow earthen channels, while wells sunk into the same water table supply the town itself. The flow of the springs has weakened over the generations, and the dry basin beside the windmill pump at the edge of the main street is a reminder that the water once stood closer to the town than it does today.</p>
<p>The channels feed the palmery, a broad grove of date palms spreading south and east of the town and making up the greater part of the oasis. It is farmed in three tiers in the manner common to the oases of the Asef: the tall date palms form a canopy that shades the ground from the desert sun, fig and other fruit trees grow in the cooler air beneath them, and the lowest level is given over to vegetables, grain and fodder crops for the animals of the town. Without this shade very little could be grown at all, and the palmery is several degrees cooler than the open desert around it. Water is not owned outright but divided among the growers by time, each plot receiving the flow of the channels for a fixed number of hours in turn, a custom that is older than the mines and remains a frequent source of quarrels in the town.</p>
<p>The Auroch herders of Nabil depend on the oasis as well, grazing their animals on the fodder of the palmery and on the scrub that grows along its outer edges, and driving them further into the desert only in the short season after the rains. Beyond the palmery the ground turns quickly to sand, and the dunes to the south and east of the oasis are slowly advancing on its outer groves, held back only by palm frond fences and the constant labour of the growers.</p>

<h2>Layout</h2>

<p>Nabil is laid out along a single main street of packed sand running north to south, crossed near its northern end by a broader road that follows a dry, stone lined watercourse and carries the caravan traffic toward the north. The buildings on either side of the main street are low timber structures with shingled roofs and raised boardwalks, set back behind rows of hitching posts and water troughs where mounts are left in the shade. A stone well with a hand crank stands at the crossing of the two roads and remains the town's most dependable public source of water within the town, while a windmill pump on the southeastern edge of the street feeds a cistern and a shallow stone lined basin that lies dry for much of the year. The southwestern corner of the street is taken up by a fenced corral and stacks of hay for the animals of the caravans and herders. Beyond the last buildings the town gives way quickly to scrub, cactus and the open desert.</p>

<h2>Economy</h2>

<p>The primary reason for Nabil's existence in its present form is the copper trade. The Dustlicker Shafts lie roughly three hours west of the town and are owned by the Shari of Bema, an old elven bloodline of the kind that comes closest within the Sultanate to an ancient house of dynastic nobility. No member of the Bema resides in Nabil itself. The family's business in the region is instead handled by Chief Sokan, who oversees the mines and conducts the dealings of the Bema with the town, its merchants and the caravans that carry the ore away. In the absence of any resident Sultanate official of note, Sokan's position as the representative of the mine owners gives him a weight in local affairs that extends well beyond the shafts themselves.</p>
<p>Caravans leave Nabil heading north, either northwest toward Ikara or northeast toward Al-Kis, carrying copper from the Dustlicker Shafts to the markets of the northern Asef and returning with the supplies, tools and labour on which the mines depend. Beyond the mining trade, the waters of the oasis support date and fig farmers whose groves form the green heart of the settlement, a few herders who keep <a href="Auroch" class="internal-link">Aurochs</a> on the sparse grazing around the town, and a small number of artisan craftsmen working for the needs of the town and the passing caravans.</p>

<h2>The Waterskull Saloon</h2>

<p>The most important building in Nabil is the Waterskull Saloon, a three storey building standing at the centre of the town near its main and only intersection. As the largest structure in the settlement, the Saloon serves as tavern, lodging house and the unofficial meeting place of Nabil, where miners spend their wages, caravan masters strike their bargains and news from Ikara and Al-Kis first arrives in town.</p>
<p>The ground floor is a single long hall entered through double doors from a covered front porch. A worn red runner leads from the doors down the centre of the room to the bar at its far end, above which hangs the great bleached skull of an <a href="Auroch" class="internal-link">Auroch</a>, its long horns spread across the back wall among the bottles. The skull is commonly held to be the origin of the Saloon's name. The floor on either side of the runner is filled with round tables where most of the town's card games are played, and a piano stands against the western wall. A small kitchen is built onto the back of the building beside the bar, and a staircase in the corner of the hall leads to the floors above.</p>
<p>The upper floor is built as a gallery around an open well above the main hall, so that those seated at its tables can look down on the room below. Large chandeliers made from old cart wheels hang from the ceiling into the well and light both floors in the evening. A row of guest rooms runs along the rear of the building, the largest of them furnished considerably more comfortably than the others, and a balcony across the front of the building looks out over the main street. An outside staircase and walkway along the western wall give access to the upper floor without passing through the hall.</p>
