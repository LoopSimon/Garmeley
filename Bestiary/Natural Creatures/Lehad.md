<p>The Lehad is a large flightless waterfowl found in the marshlands, river deltas and lake margins of <a href="Vihr" class="internal-link">Vihr</a> and Weadlands watery areas in northern <a href="Meadland" class="internal-link">Meadland</a>. From a distance, or glimpsed through marsh reeds, a Lehad resembles a very large goose or domestic waterfowl, white and blue-grey plumage, webbed feet, the rounded body of a bird that spends its time on water. The three heads become apparent at closer range. Each rises on a long serpentine neck from the same body, each is covered not in soft feathers but in bare scaly reptilian skin, and each carries a jaw full of sharp curved teeth entirely unlike the bill of any waterfowl. All three heads appear to operate with a degree of independence, moving separately, tracking different directions, occasionally appearing to argue with each other over a piece of food, though they coordinate with disturbing effectiveness when the creature is threatened or hunting. The collective screaming of all three open mouthed heads simultaneously has been described by those who have heard it as one of the more memorable sounds produced by any living creature in Ilrakhan.</p> 

<p>Lehads are omnivores in the fashion of waterfowl generally, they eat fish, amphibians, water plants, carrion, and anything small enough to be seized by one of the three heads before the other two can interfere, but their toothed jaws and aggressive territorial temperament make them considerably more dangerous than their dietary habits suggest. A nesting pair will defend a substantial stretch of marsh with a ferocity that Vihri wetland communities have learned to respect; several Vihri folk tales feature Lehads in the role of guardians of particular lakes or rivers, their three headed nature interpreted in older traditions as an embodiment of past, present and future watching simultaneously. Whether this association preceded or followed the adoption of three-headed imagery in certain Vihri decorative traditions is unknown. Hunting Lehads for their plumage, whose unusual blue white colouring has made it a valued material in certain textile traditions, is considerably more dangerous than the size of the bird suggests it ought to be.</p>
