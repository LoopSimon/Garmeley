<small><i>Unðimaðr of the northern Seas</i></small>
</div>

<p>The Unðimaðr, rendered variously in surface-language accounts as "the being beneath," "the under creature" or simply "the thing from under" depending on the translation, is amongst the most feared creatures in the maritime folklore of the <a href="Bykmåli" class="internal-link">Bykmåli</a> of <a href="Trykkerog" class="internal-link">Trykkerog</a> and the island and coastal communities of the northern seas, and has been sighted with sufficient consistency across a wide enough area that the question of whether it exists has been settled to most scholars satisfaction. It presents as a large, low slung quadruped, somewhere between the size of a bear and a large horse, with a body of dark brown black fur, a back covered in dense sharp spines, a long whip like tail with fin like fringing, and a head that is unmistakably that of a deep sea fish: blue scaled, spotted, one large white eye, two curved horns, and a jaw of forward facing teeth that opens wider than the rest of the head suggests it should. It moves on land with the purposeful ease of a creature that finds the transition from water to ground unremarkable. In water it reaches speeds that sailors who have seen it from above describe in terms that learned authors find difficult to reconcile with any known large aquatic animals hydrodynamics.</p> <p>The Unðimaðr is documented primarily from the far north, the cold seas around Trykkerog, the northern passages, the waters where <a href="Bykmåli" class="internal-link">Bykmåli</a> fishing communities have lost boats and crew to things large and fast enough that the vessel was gone before anyone on shore understood what was happening. It has been sighted as far south as the Torn Sea, though whether these represent the same population ranging widely or separate communities is unknown. Bykmali oral tradition treats it not as an animal but as a category of supernatural malevolence, the specific evil that lives directly beneath the keel of any boat at sea, patient and present, waiting for the moment the water becomes shallow enough to pursue a person onto land. Whether this theological framing is a misinterpretation of a very dangerous animals behavior or reflects something about the Unðimaðrs intelligence and intent that conventional natural history cant quite grasp is unclear.</p>
