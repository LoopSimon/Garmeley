<p>The Ganbeast is, by the assessment of the few naturalists who have managed to observe one at close range without it drifting serenely away into the forest canopy, one of the more improbable animals in the known world. It grows to four metres in length, carries a body of dense grey white shaggy fur from which large fern like fronds grow as naturally as hair, sports several spiraling horns along its back and shoulders that curve outward in patterns reminiscent of nautilus shells, and possesses a massive serpentine tail that it holds curled upward behind it like a question mark. Its large yellow red eyes give it an expression of perpetual mild astonishment. It lives in the crowns of the largest trees of the dense forests of western <a href="Behera" class="internal-link">Behera</a> — in the lands of <a href="Bilsworth" class="internal-link">Dandry</a>, <a href="Geol" class="internal-link">Geol</a>, the lands of Palmunia and the Lameidus and on the islands of the Smoking Sea, where it feeds on fruit, nuts and leaves and pursues no other agenda. The spiraling horns are theorized to be the organ through which it manipulates gravity, an innate organic magical capacity that allows it to drift through the air at surprising speeds, make jumps that should not be possible for its mass, and navigate the upper canopy with a dexterity that no creature of up to four metres should reasonably possess.</p> <p>The Ganbeast is entirely harmless. This fact has not prevented it from accumulating a considerable folklore of malevolence in the regions it inhabits, where its size, nocturnal habits and uncanny aerial movement have given rise to accounts of demon creatures haunting the forest crowns. The actual animal mates for life, lives in small family groups within a territorial section of the upper canopy, and has been reported to reach up to  two hundred years of age, though this figure derives from accounts of individuals observed across multiple human generations rather than from any controlled study, as the Ganbeast does not survive well in captivity. It is rarely encountered and occasionally disbelieved entirely by those who have not seen one. Scholars who have encountered it in the field tend to describe the experience with a particular quality of bewilderment that the creature seems to inspire reliably. The specific disorientation of encountering something that looks like a myth made to consume one in a single bite, and then discovering it is simply eating a fig.</p>
