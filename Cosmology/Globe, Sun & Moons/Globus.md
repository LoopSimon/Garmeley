<p>The Globus, sometimes called the Globe, is the world on which all the known peoples live: a large, heavy planet with a thick atmosphere, two moons in its sky, and a year of 400 days. It circles <a href="The Sun" class="internal-link">the Sun</a>, and to the planar theorists it is the seat of the Material Realm (see <a href="Material Realm" class="internal-link">Material Realm</a>).</p>

<p>The Globus is about 51,000 km around at the equator and about 16,200 km across, with a surface gravity of 1.27 g. Everything on it, from a falling stone to a breaking wave, strikes harder than it would on a lighter world.</p>

<h2>Year, Day and Seasons</h2>
<p>A day on the Globus lasts 24 hours and a year 400 days, which the Common Calendar divides into ten months of 40 days (see <a href="Calendars" class="internal-link">Calendars</a>). The world circles the Sun at an average distance of about 159 million km, on a slightly stretched path, and its axis is tilted by 30 degrees, so that the seasons are strongly marked, though the thick air softens them.</p>
<p>Because the path is stretched, the world is nearer to the Sun at one time of the year than at another. At present it is closest in the southern summer, which gives the south short, fierce summers and long cold winters, and the north the gentler year. Over tens of thousands of years the hemispheres trade places. The axis also wobbles slowly in a great cycle, the precession of the Globus, which <a href="Tripparch" class="internal-link">Tripparch</a> of Nyleikos was the first to measure.</p>

<h2>Air and Climate</h2>
<p>The air of the Globus is thick, about 1.8 atmospheres at the surface, and is made up of 86 percent nitrogen, 13 percent oxygen and 1 percent argon, with a trace of carbon dioxide. This heavy blanket warms the world to an average of about 15 degrees Celsius in spite of the Sun's weaker light. It also makes the air itself more forceful: water boils at about 117 degrees, the wind pushes harder, and storms are fiercer. In the mountains altitude sickness begins at about 2.5 to 3 km.</p>

<h2>The Magnetic Field</h2>
<p>Deep in the Globus, slow currents in its liquid iron core raise a magnetic field that reaches far out into the sky. The field turns aside most of what streams from the Sun and from beyond, which would otherwise strip the high air and bathe the ground in radiation, and the thick air adds a second shield. The field is not steady. Every few tens of thousands of years it falls into a weak phase and for a few centuries stands at a fraction of its strength before it recovers. Nor is it even over the world: beneath eastern Behera lies a broad region where the field has long been weaker than anywhere else, set there by the way the core flows below it, and there more of what falls from the sky reaches the ground. The last weak phase was about 25,000 years ago, in the same centuries as the <a href="Nawiunic Event" class="internal-link">Nawiunic Event</a>.</p>


<h2>Seas and Tides</h2>
<p>The seas of the Globus are rough. The thick air drives them harder, wide oceans give the swells thousands of kilometres to build, and every wave strikes with the weight of the world's gravity behind it. The tides, by contrast, are gentle. The Sun raises a tide about two fifths stronger than that of <a href="Luna Major" class="internal-link">Luna Major</a>, so that high water comes at about the same hour each day, and the highest tides come every twenty days, at the new and the full moon. <a href="Luna Minor" class="internal-link">Luna Minor</a> is far too light to raise any tide at all.</p>

<h2>The Sky</h2>
<p>The sky of the Globus holds the Sun, about half a degree wide, two moons and a great many stars. Luna Major, the great moon, runs through its phases in 40 days, and Luna Minor, a small rubble moon, circles it as a bright point of light, so that most peoples count two moons although only one of them circles the Globus. <a href="Malham" class="internal-link">Malham</a> stands above the northern pole as the North Star, and in the far south sailors steer by <a href="Tholmir" class="internal-link">Tholmir</a>. Every year the Globus crosses the dust of the Tis, and every few centuries the comets of the Tis return (see <a href="The Tis" class="internal-link">The Tis</a>).</p>

<h2>Age and History</h2>
<p>The Globus formed about 4.55 billion years ago, a little after the Sun and some fifty million years before Luna Major. The oldest scars of the sky date from the age of its second moon: about 120,000 years ago a great asteroid struck Luna Major and left the debris that became Luna Minor, and about 75,000 years ago the comet called the Tis broke apart over the world and fell on the moon and the planet in pieces. The Tisrock, the largest piece to reach the Globus, ended the Age of Roil in Aisligos (see the <a href="Complete Timeline" class="internal-link">Complete Timeline</a>). About 25,000 years ago a star burst in the sky some three hundred light years away and washed the world in light and radiation, most fiercely in the caves of eastern Behera (see <a href="Nawiunic Event" class="internal-link">Nawiunic Event</a>).</p>

<h2>The Lands</h2>
<p><a href="Inara" class="internal-link">Inara</a> is the largest continental area of the Globus, made up of the continents of <a href="Ilrakhan" class="internal-link">Ilrakhan</a>, <a href="Nirydia" class="internal-link">Nirydia</a> and <a href="Behera" class="internal-link">Behera</a>, together with the great northern island of <a href="Trykkerog" class="internal-link">Trykkerog</a>, which is generally counted as part of Ilrakhan. The large islands of Durennis and <a href="Il Haena" class="internal-link">Il-Haena</a> are sometimes counted as subcontinents of their own, though Il-Haena traditionally belongs to Behera.</p>
<p>Far to the west of Ilrakhan lie <a href="Aisligos" class="internal-link">Aisligos</a>, <a href="Peadyr" class="internal-link">Peadyr</a> and <a href="Bulug" class="internal-link">Bulug</a>, the lands of <a href="The New World" class="internal-link">The New World</a>. They are sometimes referred to as a single continent, although they are distinct landmasses.</p>
<p>In the south-western corner of the classical Ilrakhani map lie <a href="Eressa" class="internal-link">Eressa</a>, the slightly smaller landmass in the east, and <a href="Pagia" class="internal-link">Pagia</a>, the slightly larger landmass in the west. Together they are often called the Hona Continent.</p>
<p>Far to the south, beyond the Beheran Ocean, lies <a href="Sirenna" class="internal-link">Sirenna</a>, which after Inara is the largest single landmass in the world, together with the island of <a href="Bricea" class="internal-link">Bricea</a> to its west. The two are the southern New World, of which the peoples of Inara learned only in the 920s (see <a href="The Discovery of Sirenna" class="internal-link">The Discovery of Sirenna</a>).</p>
<p><a href="Zefellis" class="internal-link">Zefellis</a> lies far in the northern hemisphere. It is often considered the smallest of the continents, if not by far the largest island on the Globus, and its north is made up of uninhabitable frozen wastes.</p>
