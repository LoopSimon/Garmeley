<p>Luna Major is the great moon of the <a href="Globus" class="internal-link">Globus</a>, the larger and brighter of the two moons that the peoples of the world name. It is about 3,470 km across, a little over a fifth of the width of the Globus, and circles it at an average distance of about 592,000 km. One orbit takes about 36 days, but the Globus moves on around the Sun in the meantime, so the phases take longer, and the moon runs from new to new, or from full to full, in 40 days. That is the length of a month of the Common Calendar, ten to the year, and the Alder Calendar is built on the same cycle (see <a href="Calendars" class="internal-link">Calendars</a>). Luna Major is almost as old as the Globus itself, some 4.5 billion years.</p>

<h2>In the Sky</h2>
<p>Seen from the Globus, Luna Major is a little over a third of a degree wide. Even at the full its light is pale, enough to find a road by, and the nights under it are darker than many peoples would wish. Against the stars it moves about ten degrees a day, a little more than its own width every hour, so that anyone who watches it through a night will see it leave a star behind. At its side it carries a bright point of light, <a href="Luna Minor" class="internal-link">Luna Minor</a>, which never strays more than a few of the moon's own widths from it.</p>

<h2>The Near Face</h2>
<p>Luna Major turns once for every orbit, and so it always shows the same face to the Globus. No one on the planet has seen the other side.</p>
<p>The face that never turns away carries a scar. About 120,000 years ago a great asteroid struck the near side of the moon and left a crater more than 100 km across, with bright rays running far out over the surface. At this distance the crater itself is too small to be seen as a circle, but the rays show as a bright spot with streaks across the moon's face, always in the same place. Rays of this kind stay bright for a very long time, and the scar still looks fresh. The debris blown out of the crater gathered into <a href="Luna Minor" class="internal-link">Luna Minor</a>.</p>

<h2>The Far Face</h2>
<p>The other side never turns toward the Globus, and no one on it has seen what lies there. A second scar marks it, smaller than the first: a crater 20 to 30 km across, made about 75,000 years ago by the main body of the Tis, the comet whose smaller pieces fell on the world (see <a href="The Tis" class="internal-link">The Tis</a>).</p>

<h2>Eclipses and Tides</h2>
<p>Seen from the Globus, Luna Major is smaller than the Sun, about two thirds of its width. When it crosses the Sun's face it cannot cover it completely and leaves a bright ring, so the world knows annular eclipses only and no total eclipse of the Sun. When the moon itself passes into the shadow of the Globus the eclipse is total.</p>
<p>The moon is far away and not heavy, and its tides are weak. The pull of the Sun on the seas is stronger than its own, so that high water comes at about the same hour every day, near noon and midnight, and does not slip later each day with the moon. The highest tides come around the new and the full moon, every twenty days, and the weakest between them, at the half moons. The seas of the world are rough all the same, but it is the thick air that drives them, and not the moon.</p>

<h2>In Qalmyr</h2>
<p>To the Orcs of the <a href="Qalmyr" class="internal-link">Qalmyr</a> faith the moon is <a href="Qastor" class="internal-link">Qastor</a>, the Pale Father, and he is male. They read the scar on his face as the wound where <a href="Zolra" class="internal-link">Zolra</a> was torn from him in the <a href="Ikval" class="internal-link">Ikval</a>, and the small bright moon that circles him as the one thing he cast away that can never leave him. The elves call the moon Milin.</p>
