<p>Luna Minor is the small moon of the world, a loose heap of rubble about 10 km across on average, roughly 12 by 10 by 8 km, that circles <a href="Luna Major" class="internal-link">Luna Major</a> rather than the <a href="Globus" class="internal-link">Globus</a> itself. It is a young moon, formed about 120,000 years ago from the wreckage of a great impact on Luna Major, and it is the only moon in the sky that can be seen moving around another.</p>

<h2>In the Sky</h2>
<p>To the naked eye Luna Minor shows no disc and looks like a single point of light, a bright star at the great moon's side. Its surface is fresh and has not yet darkened, so it reflects about twice as much light as the old rock of Luna Major. Near the full moon it shines about as brightly as the brightest stars, and at the half moon like an ordinary bright star, but it never rivals <a href="Sarhen" class="internal-link">Sarhen</a>.</p>
<p>It never strays far from the great moon, never more than about two and a half degrees from it, some seven moon-widths, and it travels with Luna Major while the true stars are left behind. Because the great moon moves on as well, a full round as seen from the Globus takes a little over three days, which the peoples who count it reckon as three days, three, three, three and four, so that five rounds fill sixteen days. In a single night it can shift by as much as four moon-widths, fastest as it passes the moon, then slowing, stopping and turning back near the far end of its swing. Anyone who watches the sky will know that the moon has a companion that circles it, and so most peoples of the world count Luna Minor as a second moon, usually the small moon, although no eye can make out more than a point of light.</p>
<p>Its orbit is tilted, and so it does not simply pass in front of the moon and behind it. Most of the time it traces a narrow loop, passing above the disc of Luna Major on one leg and below it on the other. Only for a few days in every eighteen or so does the tilted orbit line up edge-on with the world, and then Luna Minor crosses the face of Luna Major, or slips behind it or into its shadow and is gone for a while before it returns on the other side.</p>
<p>It is easiest to find around the half moon, when it stands out beside the pale half disc. Near the full moon it is often lost in the glare, though its stretched orbit can carry it clear at the far end of its swing, and for about a week around the new moon it cannot be seen at night at all. Being an irregular tumbling rock, it also brightens and dims a little as it turns.</p>

<h2>Orbit</h2>
<p>The orbit of Luna Minor is stretched and tilted, because 120,000 years is too short for tides to round it out. It swings between about 14,000 km from Luna Major at its nearest and about 26,000 km at its farthest, and a true circuit takes just under three days. The same tides slowly drag the small moon inward, for Luna Major turns far more slowly than Luna Minor circles it. It is spiralling down, and will one day strike Luna Major, but not for millions of years or more, far beyond the span of any civilisation. Heavier bodies would have sunk much sooner, and Luna Minor is about as large as such a moon can be and survive.</p>

<h2>Origin</h2>
<p>About 120,000 years ago a large asteroid struck the near side of Luna Major. The blast threw up a cloud of debris, and the fragments collided and gathered into Luna Minor, so that it is made of the moon's own missing piece. The crater is the scar on the face of Luna Major that never turns away.</p>
<p>Some of the debris fell on the Globus as meteorites, and the heavy meteor showers that followed are the likely cause of the Era of Ash and Ice that began in the same age (see the <a href="Complete Timeline" class="internal-link">Complete Timeline</a>). The impact changed nothing in the orbit of Luna Major or the tides, and Luna Minor itself holds no more than a seventy-millionth of the great moon's weight, too little to move any orbit or any sea. The age of the impact is far beyond the reach of any tradition, and no people remember it.</p>

<h2>In Aisligos</h2>
<p>The Geipra of <a href="Aisligos" class="internal-link">Aisligos</a> know the small moon as the Dancing God, for the way it loops about the great moon through the night, and take it for a god that circles the world itself (see <a href="Cult of the Dancing God" class="internal-link">Cult of the Dancing God</a>).</p>


<h2>In Qalmyr</h2>
<p>The Orcs of the <a href="Qalmyr" class="internal-link">Qalmyr</a> faith know the small moon as <a href="Zolra" class="internal-link">Zolra</a>, the Ruinous Mother, and the elves call her Hidja. To the Qari she is everything that Qastor cast away in the <a href="Ikval" class="internal-link">Ikval</a>, and her bright light, forever circling him and never able to leave his side, is read as the sign of what was torn from him. The First Path takes her small size for a sign of her lesser power. The Zolraii of the <a href="Path of the Second Moon" class="internal-link">Path of the Second Moon</a> keep their feasts by her round, counted in days of three and four.</p>
