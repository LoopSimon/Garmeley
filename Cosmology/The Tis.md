<p>The Tis was a comet, and it no longer exists as one. About 75,000 years ago it came in toward the Sun, broke apart a few weeks before it reached the world, and fell on the moon and the planet in pieces. Its main body struck <a href="Luna Major" class="internal-link">Luna Major</a>, the largest piece to reach the world ruined a part of <a href="Aisligos" class="internal-link">Aisligos</a>, and the rest fell as fireballs and meteorites or missed the world altogether. What missed still circles the Sun, and the <a href="Globus" class="internal-link">Globus</a> meets its dust every year and its larger fragments every few centuries.</p>

<h2>The Breaking</h2>
<p>The Tis was a rocky comet, a mix of stone and ice rich in carbon, and not the loose snowball that burns away on the way in. The heat of the Sun cracked it a few weeks before it arrived and strung its pieces out along its path, and the Globus and Luna Major swept through that line within a few hours of each other. The main body, a block 1 to 2 km across, struck the far side of Luna Major and left a crater 20 to 30 km wide on the face that never turns toward the world. Smaller pieces, from a few metres to about ten metres across, fell on the Globus as bright fireballs, airbursts and meteorites. The rest of the comet missed and went on around the Sun.</p>

<h2>The Tisrock</h2>
<p>The largest piece to reach the world was the Tisrock, between 30 and 100 metres across, and it fell in Aisligos. The blow was terrible but local, a crater a kilometre or two wide or a blast that flattened a whole region, and the rest of the world did not feel it. In Aisligos it ended an age. The Age of Roil, the long time of elemental rule and primordial chaos, ended with the Tisrock, for the primordial beings that ruled the land died in its fire, and the land rested afterwards. After so many ages the crater is worn down, but a layer of strange rock and a scatter of meteorites still mark where it fell.</p>


<p>A comet is made of the things that living things are made of, and the Tis was rich in them: water, carbon compounds, ammonia, phosphorus and the building blocks of flesh. The Tisrock site was strongly enriched, and fragments that burst high in the air spread fine dust far beyond Aisligos. Water and the chemistry of life came to the world with it.</p>
<p>Whether it brought more than that no one can say. Only dust and small stones fell outside Aisligos, yet the <a href="Wispmer" class="internal-link">Wispmer</a> are found in many lands, and the <a href="Alder" class="internal-link">Alder</a> rose in the ruined land long after the Tisrock fell. The scholars who have noted the resemblance between the two sometimes ask whether the Tis brought only stone. Nothing has been proved.</p>

<h2>The Falling Stars</h2>
<p>The dust of the Tis lies along its old path, and the Globus crosses it every year on about the same date, so that each year falling stars are thick in the sky for some nights, all of them coming from the same patch of the heavens. The Qari call these the Nights of the Fallen (see <a href="Qinari" class="internal-link">Qinari</a>).</p>

<h2>The Broken Star</h2>
<p>The larger pieces that missed still circle the Sun together, a family of comets on one long orbit of some 260 years. They return as a cluster, one after another over a few years, so that for a time a comet is nearly always in the sky. Hundreds of returns have passed since the breaking. The last was in the years around 845PA, and the tables of the Cyclopi put the next around 1105PA, some 178 years from now, though the interval wanders by a few decades. No one alive has seen the Broken Star twice, and only the very old remember the last return, and then as children. In the schools of the west the comets were long held to be burning vapours of the high air (see <a href="Ralzig" class="internal-link">Ralzig</a>), the Rakhi astronomer <a href="Shimun" class="internal-link">Shimun</a> was the first to note that the years between the returns wander, and the return of the 840s was the first to be watched with instruments (see <a href="History of Astronomy" class="internal-link">History of Astronomy</a>).</p>

<p>The Qari read the yearly falling stars as the Fallen Qinari seen again, and the comets as the Fallen come up out of the depths to look at the sky they lost, and each of the three paths keeps the nights in its own way (see <a href="Qinari" class="internal-link">Qinari</a>). The Cyclopi of Cyraclion entered the returns of the Broken Star in their tables for generations, and Tripparch is said to have been the first to see that they were one cycle (see <a href="Starworship" class="internal-link">Starworship</a>). Among most peoples a return is the one event in the sky that belongs to a generation and not to a year. Those who see it tell of it to the end of their lives, and children born in its years are said to have been born under the Broken Star.</p>
