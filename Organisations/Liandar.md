<p>The Liandar describes both those who conduct the ancient Shirin practice of Lian, the Gelfir word for <a href="Alchemy" class="internal-link">Alchemy</a>, as well as the formal institution within the <a href="Twintail Imperium" class="internal-link">Twintail Imperium</a> that practices the Art in central Nirydia. It is seen as the highest academic calling in Shirin culture, ranked above the practice of magecraft or the runic teachings of artificery. Formally established in 755PA under the patronage of Grand Vizier Chiangal, the Liandar represents the culmination of centuries of indigenous Shirin alchemical tradition brought under unified imperial organization for the first time.</p>

<h2>The Practice of Lian</h2>

<p>The Lian can be divided into three practices: the Wudan, Pindan and Taidan. The Wudan describes the creation or infusion of matter interacting with the world, the manipulation of the external. The Pindan, opposed to it, describes the alteration of organic matter interacting with the self, the transformation of the internal. The Taidan is seen as the crown jewel and highest of the Arts, being the marriage of Wudan and Pindan, and is only practiced by the foremost teachers of the Lian. The three-tier structure reflects the Lakalist principle of paired complementary correspondences, Wudan and Pindan as the symmetrical opposition, Taidan as their consonant resolution: a framework imposed upon what was originally a more fluid and unsystematized body of craft knowledge during the early Natani period.</p>

<p>The secret crown jewel of the Taidan is the creation of the Runur, the Gelfir word for <a href="Homunculus" class="internal-link">Homunculus</a>. The Runur are purpose-built alchemical constructs possessing limited but genuine capability — loyal, functional, and possessed of sufficient responsiveness to serve as an elite protective force. They are used as guards within the Imperial Palace, answerable ultimately to the Imperator himself. The existence of the Runur is officially unacknowledged by the Twintail court, though within the Imperium their presence in the palace is widely rumoured among the administrative and scholarly classes, and their reputation as a deterrent to palace intrigue is considered by many historians to be at least partially intentional.</p>

<h2>Organization</h2>

<p>As an institution the Liandar stands firmly under the control of the Imperial Secretary within the Imperium. It is headed by the Zuanzaz, literally "The Two Minds" in Gelfir, a dualist leadership position held simultaneously by the two highest-ranking members of the institution. The dual headship is itself a Lakalist structural choice, reflecting the symmetrical correspondence principles that govern all imperial institutions, and echoes the Wudan-Pindan paired opposition at the heart of Lian practice itself. In practice the two Zuanzaz are expected to reach consensus on all institutional decisions, a requirement that has historically produced both remarkable internal coherence and, in periods of genuine disagreement between the two holders, significant institutional paralysis.</p>

<p>The Liandar's placement under the Imperial Secretary rather than under the Nan of Rites, which administers most scholarly and religious institutions within the Imperium, was a deliberate structural decision made at the time of its founding. Grand Vizier Chiangal, who established the institution, originally envisioned it as an extension of the palace apparatus answerable to the Grand Vizier's office. The subsequent transfer of oversight to the Imperial Secretary, which occurred during the reign of Yoroy VIII, reflected the Secretary's successful argument that an institution producing the Imperator's personal guard force was too strategically significant to remain under palace administration alone. The resulting tension between the Imperial Secretary and the Grand Vizier's office over institutional access to the Liandar has never been fully resolved and remains a periodic source of friction within the upper imperial administration.</p>

<h2>History</h2>

<h6>The Origins of Lian (Pre-175PA)</h6>

<p>Unlike many of the world's alchemical traditions, which can be traced to direct contact with the ancient Cyracli school of Kynosis or its Rakhi successors, the Shirin practice of Lian appears to have developed independently within the Mondi Delta and river plain. No direct transmission event has been identified in the scholarly record, and the terminology of Lian bears no meaningful resemblance to either the Cyracli vocabulary codified in Neyadastes's Codex Kynosis or the Rakhi adaptations of it. Shirin scholars of the Lakalic tradition attribute this to Lakal's direct expression of alchemical truth through the symmetry-sensitive cognition of the Shirin people; historians within the Imperium's own academic institutions take the more cautious position that the origins of Lian simply predate reliable written records and cannot be definitively traced.</p>

<p>What the earliest surviving references suggest is that knowledge of material transformation through directed energy, however it was first developed, was already present in the Shirin scholarly communities of the Greater Aatar period, practiced in informal contexts within the larger temple complexes and framed in the language of Goaric natural harmony. The Goaric traditions understanding of the world as a system of cyclical material transformation provided a congenial philosophical framework for early alchemical practice, and Lian in its pre-Lakalist form appears to have been understood as material engagement with the same natural processes the faith described spiritually. Individual practitioners operated without formal organization, transmitting their knowledge through master apprentice relationships within temple and scholarly households rather than through any institutional structure.</p>

<h6>Lian Under the Twintail, Early Natani Systematization (175PA-450PA)</h6>

<p>The unification of the Shirin under the Twintail Imperium in 175PA and the progressive imposition of Lakalist theological frameworks on all aspects of Shirin intellectual and administrative life produced the first systematic reinterpretation of Lian as a discipline. The three-tier structure that defines Lian practice to the present day was articulated during the early Natani period by scholars within the nascent Lakalist administrative class who recognized in alchemical practice the same paired complementary correspondence that Lakalist theology found throughout the natural and social world. The reframing of Lian within a Lakalist symmetrical framework gave the practice a legitimacy and coherence it had previously lacked, it was no longer the private craft of individual temple scholars but a systematized discipline whose structure expressed divine legitimacy.</p>

<p>Despite this philosophical systematization, Lian remained organizationally dispersed throughout the Natani period. Practitioners operated within existing scholarly and temple institutions rather than as a distinct body, and the Taidan, was sufficiently rare that its masters were individual figures of considerable personal prestige rather than members of any organized community. Later Liandar institutional histories claim Runur creation predates the institution's formal founding by at least a century, but these claims are difficult to verify and may reflect the tendency of institutional histories to maximize the antiquity of their most prestigious achievements.</p>

<h6>The Late Natani and Lisil Periods (450PA - 627PA)</h6>

<p>The administrative decline of the late Natani period and the chaos of the Khulki Rebellion had contradictory effects on Lian practice. On one hand, the breakdown of centralized imperial authority reduced the patronage available to scholarly practitioners and disrupted the master-apprentice transmission networks that had sustained Lian knowledge through the Natani period. Several of the tradition's most significant practitioners during this era are recorded as having attached themselves to regional noble households or provincial temple institutions rather than to any central authority, a dispersal that preserved knowledge but impeded synthesis. <br>On the other hand, the relative freedom from imperial oversight during this period may have enabled experimentation that more structured institutional contexts would have constrained the most significant advances in Taidan technique appear, from the fragmentary evidence available, to have occurred precisely during this period of organizational disruption.</p>

<p>Khulkis critique of Lakalist institutional practice as the tool of oppressive power extended to all forms of Lakalist  scholarly discipline, including the systematized Lian tradition. Several prominent Lian practitioners of the period were associated with the Khulkist movement, and the rebellions defeat produced a climate of imperial suspicion toward any scholarly community that had not demonstrably maintained loyalty throughout the conflict. This suspicion was one of the factors that shaped the eventual decision to place the formalized Liandar under imperial oversight rather than allow it to develop as an independent scholarly institution.</p>

<h6>Grand Vizier Chiangal and the Founding of the Liandar (755PA)</h6>

<p>The formal establishment of the Liandar as an imperial institution in 755PA was the primary legacy of Chiangal, who served as Grand Vizier under Yoroy VI and Yoroy VII. Chiangal was himself a practitioner of the Wudan, unusual for a figure of his political position, and appears to have maintained a sustained personal interest in Lian throughout his tenure in the palace administration. His motivations for formalizing the institution were almost certainly mixed: genuine scholarly enthusiasm, recognition of the strategic value of a loyal corps of alchemists, and the institutional ambition of a Grand Vizier seeking to expand the palace influence relative to the ministerial Honanoh.</p>

<p>The Liandar as Chiangal established it was initially conceived as a palace institution answerable to the Grand Vizier's office, with its headquarters in the palace complex at Bardrab and its primary mandate the training and organization of Lian practitioners under a formal charter for the first time. The Zuanzaz structure was the Viziers own invention, a deliberate expression of Lakalist paired correspondence that also ensured that no single individual within the Liandar could accumulate the kind of personal institutional authority that might make the body a political threat. Chiangal himself served as one of the first Zuanzaz alongside a senior Taidan master whose name the institutional record preserves as Miarat, though the precise details of the early internal organization remain incompletely documented.</p>

<p>The transfer of the Liandar from Grand Vizier to Imperial Secretary occurred during the reign of Yoroy VIII, some decades after Chiangal's death. The Secretary of that period successfully argued before the Imperator that an institution whose highest practitioners produced the palace guard force represented a strategic asset that should not remain exclusively within the Grand Viziers administrative sphere. The Liandars current placement under the Secretary is a development that its founder would almost certainly have opposed, and the historic records of this transfer accounts for the periodic friction between the Liandars scholars and successive Grand Viziers who regard the institution as rightfully belonging to their sphere.</p>

<h6>The Liandar in the Modern Era</h6>

<p>By the reign of Yoroy XIII the Liandar has been an established imperial institution for almost 170 years. The institutions relationship to the broader Shirin scholarly world is one of deliberate semi separation as the Liandar recruits from the same pool of mathematically and scientifically gifted candidates that the Nan of Rites' examination system produces, but its internal training and advancement processes are entirely its own and not subject to ministerial oversight. The most advanced Taidan work, including anything related to Runur production and maintenance, is conducted in facilities within or immediately adjacent to the Imperial Palace that are physically inaccessible to anyone outside the institutions senior ranks and the Imperial Secretary's office.</p>

<p>The open secret of the Runur's existence is managed by the Liandar and the Imperial Secretary through a policy of consistent neither confirmation nor denial that has been maintained for as long as the institution has existed. Within the court and the upper administrative class the Runur are treated as an understood reality that etiquette requires one not to acknowledge directly. Beyond the court, in the wider Shirin scholarly and merchant communities, they are the subject of persistent rumour, occasional satirical treatment in popular literature, and significant speculation among foreign observers of the Twintail court whose dispatches home often include dramatic and not always accurate accounts of the palace's silent alabaster guardians.</p>
