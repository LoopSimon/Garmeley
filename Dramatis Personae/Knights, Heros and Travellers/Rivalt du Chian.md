  <p>Rivalt du Chian was the Castellan of Arkeep and the commander of Meadraths forces during the <a href="Meadi Othiri War" class="internal-link">Meadi Othiri War</a> of 824-827PA. He is regarded by most Meadi military historians as the finest soldier the kingdom produced in the 9th century and simultaneously, by his own accounting, as the man most responsible for its greatest defeat. Born in 780PA at the Estate du Chian to parents of lower nobility within the du Chian house, a family of modest means and no particular distinction, he spent a career earning a reputation that brought him to Arkeep and then through it into <a href="Othirdon" class="internal-link">Othirdon</a>. Due Chian spent the years after the war writing a memoir that insisted the reputation was not worth what it had cost. Both assessments have followed him down the centuries.</p> <h3>Early Career and the Silver Brotherhood</h3> <p>Little is recorded of du Chians origins or early military service beyond the broad contours of a career that by his early thirties had made him sufficiently distinguished within the Meadi nobility to receive a royal commission of considerable consequence in. The Silver Brotherhood was a coalition of robber barons who had established themselves in the Lutewoods between Morelle and the Westerlands by preying on trade routes and exacting illegal tolls from merchants traveling between the western highlands and the Meadi heartland. They had resisted previous attempts at suppression by the crown and the Duke du Bregelle for years before du Chian was tasked with their elimination.</p>
  <p>The campaign that followed in 811PA was methodical as Rivalt combed through the Brotherhoods outer holdings progressively before isolating its primary seat. The Brotherhoods leader,  a man calling himself Gorhart the Cruel, was brought to personal combat during the final engagement and killed by du Chians hand, an outcome that ended the Brotherhood and demonstrated to many at Korsfall that the man who had done it was more than a competent bootlicker, but a formidable soldier in his own right. He was appointed Castellan of Arkeep within a few years of the campaigns conclusion, a position that suited both his expertise and his evident capacity for independent command in difficult terrain.</p> <h3>The Meadi-Othiri War</h3> <p>When Giselher I declared war on Cimfon in 824PA and marched the royal army into the Arvale, du Chian was forty four years old and had held the pass for several years. He understood the western approaches better than anyone in Meadraths military, and the campaigns operational conduct was effectively his from the first engagement. King Giselher and <a href="Dirneux" class="internal-link">Duke Bernard du Jera</a> fought alongside the army leading by presence rather than directed by plan, du Chian directed by plan. At the Conquest of Skyroar, he bested the Cimfoni commander Sir Berengar Harffan in personal combat during the castles fall and chose to spare him, a decision that appeared merely chivalrous at the time and proved practically significant three years later. At the Battle of Arvale, he deployed the professional Meadi cavalry that shattered Herz Ascar of Irin's Locni coalition in a disciplined efficiency that made the campaigns early progress look inevitable.</p> <p>It was not inevitable. The terrain west of Skyroar offered the Meadi army none of the open ground that its cavalry required, and the locni nobilitys capacity to sustain a grinding attritional harassment across nearly two years of the Long March demonstrated that the conventional military superiority du Chian had deployed at Arvale was of limited utility in highland fighting against a people who understood the land and were fighting for it. When the <a href="Lissvalley" class="internal-link">Lissvalley Federation</a> entered the war in 825PA with archers and longbowmen, the nature of the resistance changed again. Du Chian managed these months without losing an engagement he could have won, but the strategic objective, Ballina and the Visian Sea, remained far away and the army was diminished. The memoir he later wrote makes it painfully plain that he understood his position at the time. He led into the Battle of Red Oak anyway, because there was nothing else to do and his betters wished for it.</p> <p>Du Chien himself drove deep into the Othiri line and was fighting through it when he was pulled from his horse in the press, dragged down by the weight of numbers rather than bested by any individual opponent, the leader of the meadi army was thought dead. He was in fact a prisoner of the Cimfoni, held for approximately a year before being exchanged in 828PA for Sir Berengar Harffan, the same man he had spared at Skyroar years earlier. The symmetry has been noted by historians since, albeit Due Chien himself cared little for such notions. He returned to Meadrath to a reception that embarrassed him for reasons the memoir articulates at length.</p> <h3>Of War and Shame</h3> <p>The domestic crisis that followed the wars conclusion, known to posterity as the Fiseux Uprising, and Archduke Platis du Bregelle's attempt to seize the throne from the crippled Giselher I,  placed du Chian firmly among the king's supporters. His exact role in the events surrounding Platis's arrest and execution is not clearly documented; the historical record holds him in the loyalist camp and his memoir, which is otherwise notably candid, addresses the episode in a single paragraph that tells the reader less about what du Chian did than about his opinion of those who had chosen otherwise. It is enough to know that when the crisis resolved and the crippled Kings reign continued, du Chians standing with the crown was secure.</p> <p>He returned to Arkeep and in the years that followed wrote "Of War and Shame", the primary Meadi account of the western campaign and one of the more unusual documents in the tradition of military memoir. Its unusual quality is not its literary merit, which is considerable, but its refusal of certain narrative conventions of the genre. Du Chian does not distribute blame. He does not identify the moments where better decisions by others might have changed the outcome. He identifies, with considerable precision and apparent genuine conviction, every decision he made that he considers to have been wrong, and he reconstructs each one in enough detail that the reader can see exactly what he saw when he made it and understand why he now considers it a failure. The result is a document that reads less like a generals account of a campaign than a craftsmans annotated catalogue of his own errors. It made him famous across Meadrath in a way the war itself had not, because people who had no particular interest in western military campaigns found themselves reading it. It remains in circulation.</p> <p>Du Chian continued at Arkeep into old age. When the <a href="War of Horn and Fang" class="internal-link">War of Horn and Fang</a> began in 851PA  he was seventyone years old. Whatever he made of watching a new generation march to a different war is not recorded. He died at the Estate du Chian in 861PA, aged 81, of a fever the same year Muriel von Gloss defeated Viagoris at Felhome in a war being fought over different ground entirely, for different prizes, by princes who had been children when he crossed through Arkeep.</p>
