<p>Guido Maria di Battvia, Prince of Battvia from 788PA to his death in 809PA, is remembered in Battvian history with the unambiguous contempt reserved for a ruler whose personal failings produced consequences large enough to outlast him by generations and forever shadow his lines legacy. Born in 763PA and inheriting the throne from his father Prince Anselmo I at the age of twenty five, he came to power as the beneficiary of a realm whose prosperity rested on the commercial wealth of the Cities of the eastern Bay and the constitutional arrangement, the Great Privilege that Anselmo I his fathers father,  had crafted to balance crown authority against the commercial cities' growing power. In 792PA, four years into his reign, Guido declined to submit his marriage arrangements to the Grand Council's approval as the Privilege required, choosing a local noble over the northern candidate the council had sanctioned. The cities rose in revolt. The <a href="Battvian Civil War" class="internal-link">Battvian Civil War</a> that followed lasted eight years and ended with the <a href="Paper Cities" class="internal-link">Paper Cities</a> independent, the Bay of Butera severed from Battvian sovereignty, and Guido signing a peace settlement under pressure from his brother Dario, the later Prince Dario I, and the remaining royalist nobility who had concluded that the war their Prince had started could not be won. He was thirty-seven years old and had started a war over a marriage, lost the foundations of Battvian economic power and would go on to produce no heir of his own.</p> <p>The nine years that followed the peace were a study in the degradation that awaits rulers who lose wars of their own making and are not removed from power quickly enough to be forgotten. Guido II continued to reign over a diminished realm whose nobility had no mechanism to depose him and no enthusiasm for defending him, regarded by contemporaries on both sides of the conflict with a contempt that his conduct during the war had fully earned. The cities remembered what his commanders had done at Fiarco and Fierra, and the royalists remembered that his blind eye to cruelty had widened the coalition against him. His treachery at Posi had made peace impossible until the battlefield made it unavoidable and forever brought shame upon his name. Guido died in 809PA of syphilis, aged forty-six, leaving the throne to his brother Dario and no other legacy of note. The Cevoli literary tradition, which has never been particularly restrained in its judgments of the powerful, has found his cause of death and his cause of failure too symmetrical to resist commenting upon. He is the worst Prince in Battvian history by the assessment of every historian who has considered the question, and no one has seriously argued otherwise.</p>
